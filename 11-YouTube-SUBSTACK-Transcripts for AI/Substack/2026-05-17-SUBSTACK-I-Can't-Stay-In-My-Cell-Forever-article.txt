I Can't Stay In My Cell Forever
Please Help Me
Devin A. Giordano
May 17, 2026
I’ve been incarcerated for 11 and a half years. During that time, I have dedicated my life to personal betterment, service, and creating meaningful opportunities for men serving long sentences in prison. My work has always been centered around filling in the gaps of the system. I have dedicated my time here to identifying what the system is lacking and then searching for ways to address those needs myself. I do this with no help from outside agencies. I do this with no help from the department. I do this because I genuinely believe it is my duty.

I am a nationally recognized journalist. I hold a degree from one of the most elite college-in-prison programs in the nation. I have served as chairman of numerous organizations and have created and facilitated multiple programs and workshops. I am also the co-founder of a nonprofit organization that was built entirely within prison walls. Over the years, I have completed more than 30 volunteer programs spanning multiple correctional facilities.

I say all of this not to brag or boast. I say it because what I am currently experiencing has nothing to do with the type of lifestyle I live. It has everything to do with the deep-rooted culture and unprofessional practices that are often normalized within this department.

Following an institutional search on Wednesday, May 13, I was removed from my dormitory and taken to a holding cell in the basement of another housing block, an area typically used to process individuals entering or leaving the facility. I stood in that cage for eight hours.

During that time, I was given a biscuit and a scoop of corn. No water. No access to a bathroom. No access to my prescribed medications.

At around 10 p.m., a sergeant escorted me upstairs to an empty cell and handed me bedding. I was given no explanation for what was happening, nor was I provided access to my personal property.

The following morning, I was issued a misbehavior report for smuggling and contraband. As I walked through the hallways for the first time since being placed in that cell, I immediately noticed a completely different energy from the one I had experienced during the previous three years. Officers who would ordinarily treat me with basic respect suddenly refused to even acknowledge me. Some sucked their teeth as I walked by.

At one point, an officer asked me, in front of roughly 20 incarcerated individuals, “How are you still in population? You got caught with two phones yesterday.”

I immediately clarified that he had his facts wrong and that I had merely been accused of possessing an unauthorized article of clothing, but by then the damage was already done. He had planted the seed that I had somehow manipulated my way out of what would ordinarily be considered a serious offense.

A few minutes later, while explaining the situation to another officer, a nearby officer who overheard the conversation interrupted and stated that I shouldn’t be writing about things happening in prison a clear indication that this situation was, at least in part, retaliation for my writing and advocacy.

Later that same day, a sergeant with a history of questionable conduct was reportedly walking through one of the housing units telling incarcerated individuals that I was “ratting on guys to save my ass.” That narrative quickly spread throughout the population.

When I heard what was being said, seeing no other option, I locked into my cell and tried to figure out what to do next.

Then came Saturday morning.

At roughly 8:45 a.m., I was called out of my cell to medical to receive my weekly medication. The first person I saw when I stepped out of my housing unit was the same sergeant who had spread those rumors days earlier. He was standing in the hallway talking with another officer. The moment they saw me, they stopped speaking and stared directly at me with menacing expressions.

The stares continued as I walked down the hallway.

When I exited the clinic a short time later, I observed the same sergeant standing with what appeared to be four or five officers waiting nearby. Every officer was wearing purple latex gloves.

My heart immediately started pounding.

I had no choice but to walk past them because it was the only route back to my cell, the one place in prison that offers even the slightest sense of security. As I approached them, I intentionally avoided eye contact. I figured even looking in their direction might escalate the situation further.

Out of panic, I asked one of the officers, “Do you want me to get on the wall?”

His response was a dry, “Nah, you’re good.”

Another officer standing a few feet away chuckled and added, “Yeah, for now.”

Later that afternoon, at around 2 p.m., I was called to the package room. I honestly did not want to go considering everything that had already happened, but I also did not know whether the package contained perishable items, so reluctantly, I went.

The first face I saw when I entered the area was the sergeant who had written my misbehavior report. His face registered surprise when he saw me. As he walked away and entered the hallway, he looked back over his shoulder and appeared to make a gun gesture in my direction.

After receiving my package, I noticed the other sergeant, the same one involved in the earlier encounter that morning, standing in the doorway smoking a cigarette while staring at me out of the corner of his eye.

Nearby, I saw another officer wearing a body camera that happened to be turned on, so I deliberately moved closer to him. I stayed within view of that camera in case the sergeant decided to escalate the situation further.

When I returned to my cell, I immediately called my family to document the events, just as I’ve been doing throughout the week.

Since then, I have remained barricaded inside my cell.

My family has contacted the facility watch commander numerous times. They have written letters and emails to elected officials, reached out to law schools and civil rights organizations, and tried every avenue they can think of. So far, it feels like none of it is making a difference.

And the truth is, I cannot stay in this cell forever.

If you are reading this, I am asking you to help. Due to the Sargent repeatedly spreading the narrative that I am a ‘rat,’ combined with the fact that I am currently being housed on an intake company, my safety is being placed at serious risk from both incarcerated individuals and staff alike.

Please call Commissioner Daniel F. Martuscello at 518-457-8134 and request an immediate emergency transfer for Devin Giordano #17A1451 at Eastern New York Correctional Facility.

You can also contact:

Erik M. Dilan

Chair of the Assembly Committee on Correction

DilanE@nyassembly.gov

Senator Julia Salazar

Salazar@nysenate.gov

Please request the same immediate intervention and emergency transfer from their offices as well.

Right now, visibility and public pressure may be the only things preventing this situation from escalating further.
