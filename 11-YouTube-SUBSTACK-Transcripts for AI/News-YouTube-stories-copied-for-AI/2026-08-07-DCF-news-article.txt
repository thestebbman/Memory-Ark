New Massachusetts law overhauls reporting for child welfare system
by: Ashley Shook

Posted: Aug 7, 2026 / 03:11 PM EDT

Updated: Aug 7, 2026 / 03:11 PM EDT

DCF must now publish annual reports and quarterly performance profiles.

SHARE
BOSTON (WWLP) – Gov. Maura Healey signed An Act Enhancing Child Welfare Protections into law, a measure that establishes a Foster Child Bill of Rights and overhauls reporting requirements for the state’s child welfare system.

The legislation aims to increase transparency and accountability while expanding community-based support services for families in Massachusetts. The law represents a major update to the state’s child protection framework, emphasizing educational stability for foster children and modernization of the Child Requiring Assistance process.

It expands the authority of the Office of the Child Advocate and mandates that the Department of Children and Families provide more detailed public data on its performance and regional goals.

To increase transparency, the Department of Children and Families (DCF) is now required to publish an annual report and quarterly profile reports. These documents will track performance regarding transition-aged youth and foster care reviews through online dashboards. The department must also develop a five-year strategic plan that outlines measurable goals for child safety and permanency across every region of the state.


Gov. Maura Healey emphasized that the law is designed to provide safety and early intervention. “Every child deserves the opportunity to grow up safe, supported and set up to succeed and every family deserves somewhere to turn before a challenge becomes a crisis,” Healey said. “We’re building one of the strongest child and family support systems in the country by strengthening protections for children, expanding community-based support for families and making our child welfare system more transparent and accountable.”

Lieutenant Gov. Kim Driscoll noted that the reforms aim to help families before they reach a point of emergency. “Families across the country are facing challenges and hardships and we see the impact it has on families and communities in our state,” Driscoll said. “This bill will reduce barriers for families and help families access support before challenges become a crisis.”


The new Bill of Rights for children in DCF care ensures individuals are treated with dignity and protects their rights to privacy, health care and education. It also guarantees that foster children can maintain sibling connections and participate in decisions regarding their own care. Older youth will receive specific support for transition planning, including access to personal documents and contact with attorneys.

The law updates the Child Requiring Assistance (CRA) process by focusing on community resources rather than immediate court involvement. The process is generally limited to children between the ages of 12 and 18 who are not in state custody. Most parent- or school-filed court cases are now required to first connect with a Family Resource Center to access services before a case can proceed in court.

To improve educational stability, the legislation formally establishes a DCF Education Unit and requires the Department of Elementary and Secondary Education to adopt regulations allowing children in foster care to enroll in school without delay. Under these rules, schools must gather necessary student records after the enrollment process is complete. Secretary Kiame Mahaniah for Health and Human Services and a practicing physician, said healthy children depend on effective preventive services and support.

Communication requirements have also been updated to ensure children’s attorneys are promptly informed of significant life events. DCF must now notify attorneys about placement changes, hospitalizations, arrests or significant school disciplinary actions through an automated portal. Additionally, the law ensures that abuse and harassment prevention orders issued for a child do not automatically expire when they turn 18, allowing a court to extend the protections into adulthood.


The Office of the Child Advocate (OCA) will see its authority expanded to include greater oversight of child-serving agencies and systemwide reviews. The Child Advocate will also serve as a co-chair of state and local child fatality review teams alongside the Department of Public Health. Marisol García, the Child Advocate of Massachusetts, said the law helps safeguard the rights of children in state care and moves them toward community resources. “We also thank the Legislature and Governor Healey for providing my office with additional tools to do our work and enhance our oversight,” García said.

Legislative leaders supported the measure as a way to provide transparency and stability. House Speaker Ronald Mariano said the law gives agencies the tools they need to examine problems system-wide. Senate President Karen Spilka, a social worker and legislator, noted that the tools provided to foster children, such as understanding their own rights and staying connected to their community, are profound. Senator Robyn Kennedy, Senate Chair of the Joint Committee on Children, Families and Persons with Disabilities, said the improvements will make the state a better place for children to grow.

The law establishes a special commission to study a digital education portfolio system designed to follow children in foster care throughout their schooling. The Office of the Child Advocate is also required to perform periodic reviews of child welfare data reporting as part of the new oversight measures.
