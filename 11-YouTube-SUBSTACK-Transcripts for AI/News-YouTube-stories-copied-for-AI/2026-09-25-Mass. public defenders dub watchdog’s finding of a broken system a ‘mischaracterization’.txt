By Charlie McKenna | cmckenna@masslive.com
Massachusetts’ public defender agency on Friday released a lengthy response to a report issued earlier this year by Inspector General Jeffrey S. Shapiro’s office, which found the state’s system for providing lawyers to defendants who can’t afford them was fundamentally flawed.

The Committee for Public Counsel Services acknowledged in its response that the system is imperfect. But it pushed back on the “central characterization” of Shapiro’s report, arguing that the inspector general’s conclusions are “not supported by the full record.”

Shapiro’s description “does not reflect the work of the people who built one of the nation’s most respected public defense systems,” CPCS Chief Counsel Anthony Benedetti said. “CPCS will not let a mischaracterization of its record stand unanswered.”

A spokeswoman for Shapiro’s office said it “stands by its report.”

The Legislature commissioned Shapiro’s report last summer, when it passed a supplemental budget pouring millions into the state’s public defense system.


That bill, signed into law in August 2025, allocated an extra $40 million for CPCS to hire staff attorneys.

It also gave raises to bar advocates, the private defense lawyers who handle about 80% of cases involving defendants who can’t afford representation. State law requires CPCS staff lawyers to handle 20% of such cases.

Shapiro’s report found that CPCS has regularly failed to meet that mark. It also determined that if its staff lawyers worked the number of cases they are required to, the crisis that took over the state’s courts last summer amid a coordinated work stoppage by bar advocates could have been avoided.

But CPCS’ response rejects that finding, claiming it “understated staff work for years” and was taking steps to correct the issue. During the crisis, the agency said, “staff attorneys took on substantially more work, not less, until they reached the ethical ceiling on what a lawyer can carry.”

CPCS attributed the dispute to a “basic measurement problem” with its system for documenting cases assigned to bar advocates and staff attorneys. The agency used different metrics to track the assignments, so they were not always counted using the same unit. Work to overhaul that process began before the OIG report.


And the agency reiterated its position that a “raw case count that ignores what each case requires is not evidence of capacity” because it “treats a one-day arraignment and a multiyear murder prosecution as equivalent and says nothing about the hours a case requires.”

The inspector general’s report concluded Massachusetts spends more per case than many other states with similar models. But CPCS suggested the better comparison would be whether a cheaper system would deliver comparable representation, and said national assessments have identified “significant strengths” in Massachusetts’ model.

Despite reviewing “millions of lines of CPCS billing data,” the inspector general report “does not identify a single dollar spent improperly.”

CPCS’ response also addresses what it called “among the report’s most serious” findings: that Massachusetts’ system for determining if a defendant needs a state-funded lawyer is broken.

As a result, it is “likely that a significant number of defendants have received state-funded attorneys without a verified need,” Shapiro’s report found.

“It would be wrong to believe most individuals who receive appointed counsel in Massachusetts have been properly screened,” the report noted.


CPCS did not dispute the finding, but noted that it is not responsible for verifying whether a defendant is eligible for a court-appointed lawyer — the burden actually falls on the Massachusetts Probation Service.

The agency said it supports modernizing the process and, further, argued that indigent defendants shouldn’t pay a $150 fee when they receive court-appointed counsel.

“Improving the accuracy and consistency of indigency determinations remains important, but CPCS believes the better long-term policy is to eliminate the fee rather than invest additional resources in administering it,” the response reads.

The agency’s response concludes by noting CPCS takes the inspector general’s oversight seriously and is working to address specific areas where change is warranted, like the way it counts cases and how it measures attorneys’ workloads.

But CPCS said it rejected the premise that those areas for improvement “add up to a broken system failing the clients it exists to serve.”

“CPCS ... remains committed to the measure that should govern every decision in this area: whether every person entitled to appointed counsel receives effective representation,” the agency wrote.
