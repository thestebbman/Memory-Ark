DRINKING FROM A TOILET
Getting a camera inside a county jail is impossible. Short of somehow smuggling one in and committing a crime, there's no way to see the inside without getting booked in yourself. That's why we don't have any images of the inside of Gary Blair's jail cell.

Chesterfield County deputies Tyler M. Knight and Keyshawn Peterson charged Gary Blair on Feb. 22, 2026, with impersonating a law enforcement officer.

Earlier that day, he showed up at his sister's home, and the pair got into an argument. Blair's sister, Ladonna Blair, recorded the encounter on her cell phone.

Blair was dressed in a black golf shirt with a Chesterfield County Sheriff's Office seal embroidered on the left chest, a pair of black "tactical" pants, black Under Armor [sic] boots, and a nylon duty belt containing handcuffs and pepper spray. The defendant also had a silver security badge affixed to his duty belt.

Those descriptions came directly from the arrest warrant deputies Knight and Peterson used to formally charge Blair after booking him into the county jail.

We have no way to independently verify how long Gary Blair lived in the conditions. Luckily, Blair was an avid writer.

He also suffered from severe mental disorders, and his family confirmed he'd been treated and medicated since he was 18 years old. In the days before his arrest, Blair's family said he'd had his mental health medication changed to a new, different prescription and the mixture wasn't working for him.

When he was booked into jail, his father dropped off his medication at the jail, according to his family. The family doesn't know whether jailers or the jail nurse had the prescription refilled between Feb. 22 and his death on July 24.

On April 22, 2026, exactly one month into his detention, Blair wrote a pencil-to-paper letter to his father. Some of the handwritten words are hard to decipher.

"Dear Daddy, I love and miss you a lot," is how Blair opened his six-page letter. He then went into detail about how the jail staff was treating him and other E Pod inmates. He wrote that he would fight for a jury trial, but the conditions inside the county "s--thole" have forced him to "just plead guilty and go the f--k home."

"I’ve lost about 90 pounds so far; I’m starving to death in here [sic], only two meals a day, that’s it. Breakfast and lunch [sic]; you starve, and I also have to drink from my toilet [sic] and no mattress to sleep on, only a hard ground."

"I’ve been abused by staff and assaulted by everyone on the unit; I had to go 28 days without a shower except 1+2 in 30 days. No help for a white man," Blair wrote.

Blair's letter included allegations that he and other white inmates were treated differently by jail staff, describing the jailers as "racist." Blair wrote racist epithets in his letter, as well.

"I literally starve every night; luckily there’s one white chick left. Major Jacobs [sic] she works Sun – Thurs – 5 p.m. – 11 p.m. She is cool as hell. She finally made sure I get a shower after 14 days [sic] and she got me the # to Blair’s Bonding in B-ville 4 weeks ago."

Blair's family provided a second letter to QCN. The letter was inside an opened envelope with a "Return to Sender" label stuck across the delivery address on the front. "Judge Gibbons" is visible above the post office label, and the address Blair scribbled underneath the label was not a deliverable address.

Gibbons, of course, never received the letter.

"I would like to make a statement to the court. To whom this may concern. I would like to apologize for wearing a deputy sheriff’s shirt gave to me from Sam Parker’s administration in 2011 for being his campaign advocate. I really believed in Parker and still do, because the county and jail was in great shape under Same Parker," Blair wrote in the letter dated May 15, 2026.

"I came in on the 16th of February and on the 25th of February came out of [ILLEGIBLE] isolation for the first time to do an assessment with Nurse Pam, and I received my meds my dad brought but only one [ILLEGIBLE]. I ended up in isolation 29 days and on the 29th day Troy (former jail administrator Troy Ellerbe) released me to A pod in the so-called new jail, where I stayed a week but was threatened heavily by all the inmates. The rest of the time I’ve been in E pod or P/C (protective custody) for people like me. I went 14 days without a bath, then 14 more days without a bath or shower. I was raw between the legs and ass," Blair wrote to Judge Gibbons.

Blair alleged he was physically assaulted by two inmates he named in the letter. He also accused another corrections officer of "slamming my hand up in the feeding door."

"I weighed 292 [sic]. 9 days later I weighed 270 [sic]. Rapid weight loss is very unhealthy [sic] not to mention spider bites. Drinking water from a toilet bowl since I came to E block [sic] plus my toilet wasn’t flushed for 7 days straight, then 7 more days straight. Finally cut it on and cleaned it for drinking water. The staff is very nasty to white inmates thanks to Troy and James Dixon," Blair wrote in the May letter.

"We are starved to near death and deprived basic human rights, abuse mentally physically, emotionally."

Blair wasn't the only inmate drinking from a toilet. Another inmate, Nick Glowe, who spent a week in a lockdown cell right beside Blair, was also forced to drink water from a public toilet.

Glowe was not an easy interview to schedule. Because of his embarrassment and humiliation over what he did to survive in the jail, he canceled our first attempt to interview him. Glowe later said speaking publicly about his jail stay was "dehumanizing," and not something he wanted anyone to know about. After realizing how important telling his story was to keep other inmates from going through the same things he went through, Glowe called Barr back to be interviewed for this news investigation.

The only time Glowe and other inmates would get water was between 6 a.m. and 2:30 p.m., and it was served in small disposable cups, according to Glowe.

“Pretty much breakfast and dinner. There was a couple times later at night that I asked for water. I was never, like, refused water, but unless you're, like, going out of your way to make sure that you get it, it's not really offered," Glowe told Barr in an interview in July.

"So, when you would ask for water, or if you got your breakfast and your lunch delivered to you, what did you get as far as water? The amount?" Barr asked. "We just got a little, maybe an eight- ounce cup, 12-ounce cup," Glowe said, showing the size of the cup with his hands.

"Breakfast was about 6:00, 6:30. Lunch was 10:00 to 11:00, somewhere in there, and then dinner was about 2:30 p.m. If I didn't get water at dinner time, I wouldn't have anything throughout the night," Glowe said.

"If you didn't get water at 2:30 in the afternoon, you didn't get anything until breakfast the next morning?" Barr asked. "Yeah. Once I realized my water had gone out, just being so thirsty, I actually had to drink out of the toilet. So, flush the toilet, get some fresh water coming in and scoop it out. Just enough to get by...dehumanizing and disgusting," Glowe said.

To further complicate his dehydration, Glowe was arrested on shoplifting charges for stealing wine from a dollar store. Glowe said he was struggling with alcohol addiction and stole the wine to get a fix. When he was booked into jail on June 30, he lay on the floor of his cell for days in severe dehydration and suffering from delirium tremens, "a life-threatening form of alcohol withdrawal," according to the Cleveland Clinic.

That was another reason Glowe said he convinced himself to stick his hand into a public toilet and drink from it - something Glowe said he never wanted to tell anyone he did.

"I didn't really want to tell anybody, but also at the same time there's something going on there that doesn't need to be happening to people, regardless of whatever crime you've committed. You're not going in front of a judge. You haven't actually been convicted. Like there's no reason to be treated like that," Glowe told Barr.

Glowe also corroborated Brian Smith's story of how Chesterfield County jailers treated Gary Blair at mealtime, "It’s insane, especially when they would come for meals. It would just be – he would just get sandwiches, and they'd just open the door real quick, throw them in, and then slam the door back closed," Glowe said.

"And you saw them do that," Barr confirmed with Glowe. "Yeah," he answered.

"It was another dehumanizing moment. Regardless of why he (Blair) was in there, he still shouldn't have been treated like that," Glowe told Queen City News.

Glowe said he feels lucky to have made it out of the jail alive. "A couple of times when I was getting real out of it, going through withdrawal in jail is not fun. I wouldn't recommend it, but there's a couple moments that it was like, I don't know if I'm gonna make it out of here.”

"That man shouldn't have been there. Like, it could have been a very preventable death," Glowe told Barr.

COURT FILE: BLAIR WAS MENTALLY DISABLED
In April, Gary Blair left the Chesterfield County jail for Columbia for a mental evaluation. Because of concerns about his mental health, his ability to know legal right from legal wrong, whether he'd be able to understand the court proceedings, and his ability to assist his defense counsel at trial, Blair was given a competency evaluation.

On May 12, Blair's examiners filed a report showing he was not mentally competent to face the criminal charge against him, but "is likely with treatment to become competent in the foreseeable future," according to a June 3, 2026, order signed by S.C. Circuit Court Judge Eugene Warr.

Warr ordered S.C. Office of Mental Health to provide Blair restoration treatment and authorized the state up to 180 days "for observation and treatment in an effort to restore Defendant's competence to stand trial," according to the order.

Once Blair completed treatment, the judge's order commanded the state to have Blair be re-examined to determine his competence post-treatment, then issue a report to the court, the solicitor, and to Blair's pubic defender.

The order also commanded Solicitor Mike Burch's office to immediately notify the state mental health department of the order, which Burch's office did the very next morning after the order was clocked and filed with the Chesterfield County Clerk of Court. The immediacy of the notification was to "expedite the agency's (OMH) ability to implement this order and prepare for legal proceedings," according to the order.

The Office of Mental Health is the rebranded agency formally known as the S.C. Department of Mental Health. South Carolina Department of Behavioral Health and Developmental Disabilities (BHDD) is the parent agency, and OMH operates under BHDD. S.C. Gov. Henry McMaster signed legislation creating BHDD in April 2025 with a promise to be "the state’s central agency for a holistic, streamlined, coordinated approach to care."

"The Department of Behavioral Health and Developmental Disabilities (BHDD) Office of Mental Health is dedicated to supporting the recovery of people with mental illnesses. As South Carolina’s public mental health system, it is one of the largest hospital and community-based systems of care in South Carolina. Its numerous programs, services, and divisions are designed to ensure that South Carolinians in need receive the right treatment, at the right place, at the right time from a robust, interconnected, evidence-based system of care," according to the OMH website.

It appears the right treatment at the right time did not come for Gary Blair.

Gary Blair turned 43-years-old on July 15. He was found dead inside his jail cell 51 days after Judge Warr signed the order to have Blair treated.

Queen City News inspected Blair's criminal case file on August 18. The file is the only accessible public record to look for evidence he received care in accordance with the judge's order. The file contained the June 3 order, but did not contain any document showing the Office of Mental Health took custody of Blair to treat him after the June 3 order.

OMH spokeswoman, Beth Moore, would not answer any questions about Blair's case when Barr contacted the agency the first time on August 20, asking to interview OMH leadership about why Blair was not removed from the Chesterfield County Detention Center and placed into restorative treatment in accordance with the judge's order.

"While we cannot discuss the details of any individual case, we can speak to how competency restoration works in South Carolina," Moore wrote in an August 21 email to Barr, which contained a prepared, written statement giving broad information about how her agency handles judicial orders in cases similar to Gary Blair.

"Competency restoration treatment is a legal process ordered by a court; it is not intended nor appropriate to be used as regular mental health care or acute mental health treatment. Admission follows the order in which referrals are received, and the wait time for competency restoration admission is about nine months. The only entity responsible for providing adequate medical and mental health care to the individuals in jails are the jails themselves," Moore wrote.

OMH confirmed people like Gary Blair are under a nine-month backlog for OMH to begin to carry out judicial orders of restoration treatment. That means Blair wouldn't have started his mental health treatment until March 2027.

Moore refused to schedule an interview with OMH Interim Director, Dr. Kimberly Rudd, to answer questions about what the agency has done and is doing now to shorten that nine-month backlog, telling Barr that she's "not putting" anyone on camera about this matter.

Rudd was appointed interim director of the OMH on June 1, 2026, but is also the chief executive officer and medical director of OMH's G. Werber Bryan Psychiatric Hospital in Columbia, according to Rudd's biography posted on the agency's website.

Rudd has worked for the S.C. Department of Mental Health and now the Office of Mental Health since 2008.

The OMH touts its system of treatment centers as "one of the largest hospital and community-based systems of care in South Carolina," according to its website. The agency operates "16 community mental health centers, with a combined total of 61 clinic locations, with at least one clinic in all 46 of South Carolina’s counties."

One of those clinics, Tri-County Community Mental Health Center, is located exactly one mile as the crow flies from the Chesterfield County Detention Center. The center provides outpatient mental health care.

SHERIFF: 'I don't like what I saw'
On August 20, Barr emailed Sheriff Cambo Streater to request an interview with him about the conditions Gary Blair was found in and other inmates' stories about having their water turned off inside their cells and resorting to drinking water from their cell toilets.

The sheriff declined Queen City News' interview request.

On August 24, the sheriff confirmed he went to SLED's Pee Dee Region Office for a briefing with agents; then two days later, on August 26, Streater posted a press release about changes he's made at the jail.

The first action: firing Jail Administrator Troy Ellerbe, whom Streater promoted to the post in 2023.

The same day the sheriff published his press release, instead of sitting for an interview with Queen City News to discuss what we uncovered about his jail, Streater agreed to an on-camera interview with WSOC.

“We’re transparent, and we want the public to know that we will fix what we can fix,” Streater told the station. “And if it means letting people go, we’ll let people go.”

The reporting did not include any details about Gary Blair's death or the conditions inmates were - and possibly still are - living in. WSOC reported that Streater partly blamed "what happened in July," - presumably Blair's death - on the age of the county jail.

The Queen City News investigation is the first time these details were made public in the 41 days since Gary Blair was found dead inside the county jail.

“The facility itself is a contributing factor to some of the issues we’ve had over there. It’s not totally to blame, but it is a contributing factor,” Streater told WSOC.

SLED INVESTIGATION CONTINUES
S.C. Law Enforcement Division agents walked into E Pod within an hour or so after Gary Blair's body was found. Agents photographed the condition of the jail cell and started the process to figure out who contacted Blair in the hours, days, weeks, and months before his death.

We know multiple agents are working the case because of our on-the-ground investigation inside and outside of Chesterfield County. Multiple people interviewed by SLED have confirmed being interviewed by agents, and more interviews are set to happen.

It's still unknown exactly how long Blair lay dead inside that jail cell before he was found on July 24.

The death certificate shows the Chesterfield County Coroner's Office best guess at Blair's time of death was within a four-hour window: 7:00 a.m. to 11:00 a.m. The death certificate does not show the manner of Blair's death, which is listed as "pending investigation."

The manner of death determination will come after Blair's autopsy is finished. The coroner told Blair's family that it could take up to eight weeks, which could be some time in late September.

We have confirmed with multiple sources in Chesterfield County that jailers have been interviewed by SLED agents as part of the state's criminal investigation into how Blair died and who all may have had a hand in it. Nick Glowe confirmed he was interviewed by a pair of SLED agents on August 24 at his home in Kershaw County.

Chesterfield County Jail Administrator Troy Ellerbe, who Sheriff Cambo Streater fired five days after Blair was found dead, confirmed he voluntarily interviewed with SLED agents at his home in Cheraw. Streater fired Ellerbe, but will not explain why he fired the CCSO veteran.

Barr spoke to Ellerbe by phone on August 28 seeking Ellerbe's side: "I don't know why the sheriff let me go."

Ellerbe declined to provide anything further, citing the ongoing SLED investigation and his fear of impacting the state's investigation by commenting. "I've got my own opinion, but I'm not going to talk about it at this time," Ellerbe said in the call.

Our sources have also confirmed that not all jailers agreed to be interviewed by SLED as part of the criminal investigation.

Gary Blair's family also confirmed they participated in an interview with SLED agents in mid-August. Blair's sister, Ladonna Blair, and Blair's aunt, Vivian Cassidy, drove to SLED's Pee Dee Region Office on August 13 for a formal interview.

"They said it was something that they would never forget," Cassidy told Queen City News Chief Investigative Reporter Jody Barr during an interview on August 19, the week after her SLED interview.

"I was scared at first that it was going to be somebody, you know, just push it under the rug, but the way they talked, they're not going to let it go until they get to the bottom of it. They promised," Cassidy said. "I guess they don't want to say it until they get all the evidence, they did say that it wouldn't be pushed under – that it wouldn't be forgotten, that they were going to find out what happened...I believe them.”

Cassidy and Blair had always had a close relationship. Blair was her late twin sister's son and dozens of photographs of the two together span Blair's life - from his birth to the months before he died.

Cassidy confirmed that her nephew suffered most of his life from multiple mental health disorders and had been medicated to control his disorders since he was 18-years-old. He was also a help to her and her husband around her home. She'd recently purchased Blair a car not long before his arrest, she said.

Cassidy said she grew desperate to talk to Blair in the days before his death. She said Blair wasn't allowed phone calls throughout his detention and she didn't know why. On July 22, Cassidy said she paid $30 for video visits with her nephew, then called the jail to find out why the system wouldn't let her get through.

Cassidy's phone logs show she called the jail's main line at 12:10 p.m. on July 22. She wanted to know why she couldn't get the online scheduling system to confirm a video visit with Blair. "I asked them, I said, 'I'm having an issue. I cannot get through to set up visits with Gary Blair.' And they said that I shouldn't have any issues at all. And the lady told me, she said, 'Call back in a couple of hours.”

Cassidy's cell phone call log shows she called back at 2:08 p.m.

"I talked to a guy, and as soon as I said, 'I'm trying to set up video visits with Gary Blair,' he said, 'He is not allowed visits at this time,' and that was it," Cassidy said. "And that was two days before he died," Barr asked.

"Yes," Cassidy responded.

"Do you think the reason he wasn't allowed to talk to you two days before his death is because what you now know that he was laying on a jailhouse floor dying?" Barr asked Cassidy. "Yes," she replied, "Absolutely. Terrible. Unbelievable."

We do not know a timeframe for how long SLED's investigation may take. The agency typically submits its investigative report to a prosecutor to determine whether charges are filed. In this case, Solicitor Mike Burch confirmed his office has conflicted the case out of the Fourth Circuit, meaning some other solicitor's office or the S.C. Attorney General's Office will review the investigation to determine whether to file charges in the case, and if so, who will be charged.

Cassidy said she's thankful SLED agents are working to figure out what happened to her nephew, "Thank you, thank you from the bottom of my heart. Thank you for trying to find out what happened."
