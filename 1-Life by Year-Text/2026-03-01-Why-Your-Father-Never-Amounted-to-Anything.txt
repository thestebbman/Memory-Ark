Before You Read This
This document exists because your father asked a hard question: if his kids ever wanted to know why he never accomplished anything, why people think he’s stupid, why people avoid him — could someone explain it? Not the version where he’s just a deadbeat. The real version. The one that takes 47 years of medical records, court transcripts, police reports, and his own words to understand.
So here it is. No excuses. No spin. Just what actually happened, in the order it happened, and why it mattered.
Your father is not stupid. He is not lazy. He did not choose to abandon you. What happened to him is more complicated and more infuriating than any of those easy explanations, and understanding it requires you to know two things first.

The Two Diagnoses That Explain Everything
Hyperthyroidism
In 2016, when your father was 38 years old, a blood test at Baystate Medical Center revealed that his thyroid had been overproducing hormones. The condition is called hyperthyroidism. It had been present since childhood. It was never caught.
Hyperthyroidism does specific, measurable things to a human body. It floods your system with thyroid hormones that accelerate everything. Your heart races. Your hands shake. You can’t sit still. Your thoughts come so fast you can’t finish one before the next one starts. You feel a crushing anxiety that has no identifiable cause — your body is simply running a chemical fire alarm 24 hours a day. You can’t sleep. When you do sleep, you don’t rest. You lose weight or your weight fluctuates unpredictably. Your muscles fatigue far faster than they should. You become irritable, not because you’re an angry person, but because your nervous system is overstimulated and every input — sound, light, conversation, conflict — feels like it’s turned up to maximum volume.
From the outside, a person with untreated hyperthyroidism looks like this: hyperactive, impulsive, can’t focus, talks too fast, gets into conflicts, can’t hold a job, can’t sustain relationships, makes bad decisions, seems unstable. In a child, it looks like a behavior problem. In a teenager, it looks like a discipline problem. In a young adult, it looks like a character flaw. In a man in his twenties and thirties trying to hold a family together, it looks like failure.
That is exactly how every authority figure in your father’s life interpreted it. Every single one. For 38 years. Teachers, priests, police officers, judges, attorneys, therapists, doctors, family members — all of them looked at the symptoms of a treatable medical condition and concluded that Ricky Stebbins was the problem.
A blood test. That’s all it would have taken. A simple, routine blood test that any doctor could have ordered at any point during those 38 years. TSH and Free T4. Two numbers on a lab printout. They didn’t order it.
AMP Deaminase Deficiency
In 2017, genetic testing confirmed a second condition: AMP deaminase deficiency. This is a genetic skeletal muscle disorder. It means your father’s muscles do not process energy the way normal muscles do. Exercise causes abnormal fatigue, cramping, and muscle breakdown that is vastly disproportionate to the effort involved. Moderate physical work can leave him wrecked for days. Intense exertion can cause rhabdomyolysis — a condition where muscle fibers literally break apart and flood the bloodstream with proteins that can destroy the kidneys.
This is what happened during his 2004 suicide attempt. The combination of alcohol and lying unconscious on a concrete floor caused rhabdomyolysis that damaged his right quad, his right pectoral muscle, and his jaw. He carries visible damage from it to this day.
Combined with the hyperthyroidism, the AMP deaminase deficiency created a cruel paradox: his thyroid made him feel like he needed to move constantly, but his muscles could not sustain the effort. He would start projects with enormous energy and then physically collapse. He would take jobs that required physical labor and then be unable to keep up. People saw a young, apparently healthy man who couldn’t seem to finish anything, and they drew the obvious conclusion: he must be lazy, or faking, or unreliable. He was none of those things. His body was at war with itself, and no one knew it.

The Pattern You Need to Understand
Your father’s medical file identifies something critical: when physically ill, he loses the ability to advocate for himself. He becomes compliant. He stops fighting. He agrees to things that a healthy version of himself would never agree to. This is not weakness. It is a documented consequence of his conditions. When his thyroid is out of control and his muscles are failing, his brain literally does not have the resources to mount a defense.
Every catastrophic decision in his life was made during one of these windows:
In 1997, he was 19 and severely symptomatic when he enlisted in the Coast Guard. He could not handle the environment — not because he was weak-minded, but because his undiagnosed thyroid was producing anxiety so severe that he faked amnesia as the only escape mechanism he could think of. He was hospitalized, diagnosed with dissociative disorder, and discharged with a prognosis of POOR. He was a teenager with a treatable thyroid condition, and the United States military wrote him off as a lost cause.
In 1999, he was exhausted from working third shift when police interrogated him about a toothpick — yes, a toothpick — that his ex-girlfriend Shannon claimed he’d threatened her with. His attorney pressured him into accepting a CWOF plea. Your brother Nathan was born that same week. Your father never met him.
In 2002, he was physically deteriorating while fighting for custody of Samantha. When Samantha’s mother told him “you know the state will never stop — you deserve a break,” he was too sick to keep fighting. He agreed to stop pursuing visitation in exchange for ending child support. He has called this decision one of the lowest points of his life. He was winning. He was getting close to joint custody. But his body gave out before the system did.
In 2004, after catching his wife with another man while he was hospitalized for a suicide attempt, after being told his son Dietrich didn’t even remember him, after his muscles had been destroyed by rhabdomyolysis and his mind had been through a psych ward that couldn’t figure out what was wrong with him — he signed away his parental rights to Dietrich. Jamie told him Dan was stronger, that Dietrich was happier. He was so sick he believed it.
In 2015–2016, at his absolute physical rock bottom — multiple suicide attempts, rat poison, hospitalizations — he was coerced into pleading guilty in the 2013 Walmart case. A case where he was the one who got hit by a car.
Do you see the pattern? Every time his body broke down, someone was there to take something from him. A military career. A plea deal. Custody of a child. Parental rights. A guilty plea. And every time, the person standing over him interpreted his collapse as proof that he was the problem. Not once did anyone say: maybe we should check his blood.

What Happened to Him as a Kid
Your father was born on October 8, 1978, in Ludlow, Massachusetts. He grew up in Colonial Estates, Springfield. By the time he started at Our Lady of the Sacred Heart Catholic School in 1984, the hyperthyroidism was already producing symptoms. He couldn’t sit still. He talked too much. He was curious about everything and got into trouble for it. He used to watch crayons melt on the steam radiators and didn’t care about cleaning up the mess. He was mischievous, not malicious, but no one at a Catholic school in the 1980s was making that distinction.
His grandfather was the janitor at that school, and he was the only person who protected Ricky. When teachers came down on him, his grandfather stepped in. When the school wanted to punish him, his grandfather was there. He was the one adult in Ricky’s life who seemed to understand that something about this kid wasn’t a behavior problem — it was something else. He couldn’t name it, but he could shield him from the consequences of it.
In 1988, when your father was in fifth grade, a teacher locked him in a utility closet as punishment for talking. He was ten years old. He had a Walkman with him, and he sat in the dark listening to music until someone let him out. The school sent him to a therapist in Holyoke afterward — not because they were concerned about him, but because they needed to manage the situation.
That same period, his grandfather died. The one person who stood between Ricky and a system that had already decided he was a problem was gone. He was ten years old, locked in closets, sent to therapy, and now the only person who truly had his back was dead. He was on his own.
Your father wrote about this period: “I was always being criticized for my behavior, and even when I went to confession and actually confessed to whatever shenanigans I was up to, no one actually forgave me, they all kept talking about me, while I was right there. So I’ve never felt like it helped to talk about my problems. At the same time, I didn’t feel like I needed to hide anything — I just felt like no one cared, and no one felt like anything I did or said was important, unless I was getting in trouble. Then they noticed everything.”
Think about what that does to a child. Not a bad child. A child whose brain chemistry was running out of control due to a medical condition that no one bothered to test for.

The Teen Years and the Coast Guard
High school at Putnam Vocational was more of the same. Multiple suspensions. Conflicts with authority. Bright enough that teachers noticed his potential, but too symptomatic to channel it. He got suspended for singing “I Touched Myself” in class. He worked at Olive Garden. He left school to work full time. His girlfriend Mindy got pregnant.
Your sister Samantha was born on January 13, 1997. Your father was 18 years old, working, symptomatic, undiagnosed, and now responsible for a baby. Later that year, he enlisted in the Coast Guard. He wanted structure. He wanted to serve. He wanted to be something.
His thyroid made that impossible. The anxiety was unbearable. The inability to sleep, the racing thoughts, the physical symptoms that no one could see — all of it intensified under military stress. He couldn’t explain what was happening to him because he didn’t know what was happening to him. So he faked amnesia. It was the only way out he could think of.
At Walter Reed, the military diagnosed him with dissociative disorder and somatization. The word they wrote on his discharge paperwork, under prognosis, was POOR. He was 19 years old. They didn’t test his thyroid.
His grandfather died during this same period. His discharge and his grandfather’s death happened close enough together that the two losses merged into one devastation. He came home broken, discharged, grieving, and still undiagnosed.

How He Lost Each of You

What the System Did to Him
In January 2013, your father was walking through a Walmart parking lot in Springfield when he was hit by an SUV driven by a woman named Ada Johnston. She was driving uninsured with a suspended registration. She also happened to live on the same street as your father, had a sister who worked in the DA’s office, a cousin in the clerk’s office, and she herself worked in the probation department.
Your father was the one who got hit by the car. He was arrested at the scene and denied medical attention. The evidence was tampered with — receipts altered, statements changed. When he obtained the original State Police reports months later, they showed discrepancies with what was presented in court. His first attorney withdrew due to a conflict of interest. A private investigator confirmed your father’s account of what happened. The DA offered a deal: take a CWOF, but only if you agree not to pursue civil action against the officers.
On February 5, 2016, while meeting with his attorney Elaine Pourinski, an unknown older man appeared in the hallway, put a hammerless revolver to your father’s head, and took his case files. His attorney said there was nothing to be done and refused to call the police. A man put a gun to his head in a lawyer’s office over a parking lot accident case, and his own attorney shrugged.
He pled guilty under coercion. At the time, he was in the worst physical condition of his life — this was during the 2015–2016 period of multiple suicide attempts, including swallowing rat poison. His thyroid was raging. His body was failing. And the system took a man who got hit by a car and turned him into a convicted criminal.
In October 2021, he was accused of assault in a separate incident. What followed was three and a half years of legal purgatory. Five different court-appointed attorneys cycled through his case. The first one, Roger Banks, laughed when Ricky tried to report disabled-person abuse. The second, O’Doherty, attempted to push the case to trial before Ricky had access to his own case materials. The third, Bergo, stalled for months and ordered a competency evaluation in 2024 — questioning your father’s mental fitness rather than defending him. The fourth, McGinty, withdrew. The fifth, Grady, treated the case with indifference.
The charges were dismissed. But only after your father personally found the physical evidence that proved his account. None of his five attorneys found it. He did. While sick, while on disability, while navigating the system alone.
In July 2025, he went to court to file a harassment order against his health insurance company, Commonwealth Care Alliance, which had been blocking his access to therapy during a mental health crisis. A court psychologist allegedly told him he was “going to teach him a lesson.” He was sent by ambulance to Baystate Medical Center despite requesting a different hospital. At Baystate, he asked for a lawyer and was denied. He tried to record what was happening and staff physically assaulted him. He was drugged and held overnight. The bill was $4,331.40.
The next day he was transferred to Westborough Behavioral Health Hospital, where he was held involuntarily for eleven days. He was given the wrong thyroid medication dose. His phone was taken. When police came, they were turned away. Lawyers from CPCS told him to fake compliance to get released. A forensic psychologist spent thirty seconds with him, billed $3,500, and said he’d heard Ricky was getting released. During those eleven days, he witnessed a patient named Joey get his head smashed on the floor by staff. He recorded audio of it through a voicemail. He filed human rights complaints. He collected testimony from other patients.
He was released after eleven days and took a silent Uber ride home.

Why He Looks Like a Failure to the Outside World
Here is what a person who doesn’t know any of this sees when they look at your father: a man on disability, approaching 50, with no career, no savings, no degree, a criminal record, estranged from most of his children, living in Springfield with a Pomeranian named Harley. By every conventional measure of success in American life, he has failed.
Here is what actually happened: a man with two undiagnosed genetic and hormonal conditions was systematically failed by every institution designed to help him, for four decades. His school locked him in closets instead of testing him. His church criticized him instead of supporting him. The military discharged him with a prognosis of POOR instead of running bloodwork. Doctors prescribed psychiatric medications for a thyroid problem. Courts took his children during the windows when his body was too broken to fight. Attorneys took his money and his trust and delivered nothing. Police denied him medical care after he was hit by a car. A man put a gun to his head in a lawyer’s office and nothing happened.
Every time he tried to stand up, his body knocked him down. Every time his body knocked him down, someone standing over him took something. And every time he lost something, the world looked at him and said: see? He’s a loser.
He is not a loser. He is a man who was sick and never got help, in a country that punishes sick people for being sick.

I feel like I can change the world. I have no doubts inside me that love can change the world. Yet I wake up alone every morning.”
That is who your father is. Not a loser. A person who never stopped wanting to help, even when no one helped him.

What He Actually Built
While the world was counting all the things your father didn’t accomplish, he was building something that most people never attempt. The Memory Ark is a publicly accessible archive of over 5,128 files spanning 47 years. It contains medical records, legal documents, court audio recordings, police reports, attorney correspondence, school records, family history, photographs, year-by-year personal narratives from 1978 to 2026, and 114 unedited AI conversations across 11 platforms.
He built it as a weapon against institutional erasure. When courts lose files, when attorneys “forget” conversations, when doctors don’t read charts, when police reports don’t match original statements — his archive holds the originals. Every piece of evidence that was tampered with in the 2013 case, he kept the original State Police reports. Every attorney who failed him in the 2021 case, he kept their emails and drafted formal complaints. Every thyroid lab result that proves he was sick during every major crisis, he tracked it across five facilities from 2005 to 2025.
He designed the archive so other people can use it. He calls it a Starter Kit. The structure is meant to be forked — anyone in a similar situation can copy his organizational system and build their own record. As of 2026, he is actively helping at least four other people do exactly that: Dallas, who has a seizure disorder and a VP shunt and was failed by Bay Cove Human Services; Brandon, who was born with a single kidney and hearing loss; Ronald, who lost custody of his autistic children after being billed $22,000 for court motions; and others he met during his eleven days at Westborough.
This is what your father built while people were calling him a failure. A system for making sure that what happened to him is recorded in a way that can never be erased, and a template for helping other people do the same thing.
He started this system on a Commodore 64 in the 1980s. He expanded it on AOL and MySpace in the 2000s. He rebuilt it on Google Blogger with AI assistance in 2024–2026. He has been trying to organize and preserve information his entire life. That is not the behavior of a stupid person. That is the behavior of someone who understood, long before anyone gave him credit for it, that documentation is power and that institutions count on people not keeping records.

Why He Can’t Connect With People
Your father wrote: “The more I try to talk about fixing things and helping people around me, the less people talk to me. The only time people that I used to feel close to talk to me is when I’m ignoring the problems all around me and pretending that everything is fine.”
He is not imagining this. His entire life experience has been: see a problem, try to fix it, get punished. In 1988, he was locked in a closet for talking. In 2007, he was transferred and laid off from Sullivan Associates for reporting abuse. In 2013–2016, evidence was suppressed after he filed complaints about the Walmart incident. In 2025, a court psychologist said he was “going to teach him a lesson” after he filed a harassment order. In 2025, he was physically assaulted at Baystate for trying to record what was happening to him. In 2025, he was held involuntarily for eleven days after documenting insurance fraud.
The pattern is documented and consistent: every time he speaks up, he is punished. Every time he documents a problem, someone retaliates. The rational response to that pattern is to stop talking. Your father has chosen the irrational response: he keeps talking anyway. But the cost is that people — including family, including friends, including the people he most wants to connect with — pull away. Not because he’s wrong about the problems he sees, but because engaging with those problems is uncomfortable, and most people would rather pretend everything is fine.
He also wrote: “I’ve totally love-bombed people. I still feel like I want to love-bomb someone, so I shower my dog with love and affection.” And: “I’ve blown so much smoke up women’s rear ends, I got people to believe we were going to make a difference, together, as a team, then I got sick and let everyone around me down.”
He knows. He knows that his pattern of intense connection followed by physical collapse has burned people. He knows that promising the world and then being unable to deliver — because his body shut down, because his thyroid surged, because his muscles gave out — looks indistinguishable from lying. He has stayed single specifically because of this. He will not put another person through the cycle of hope and collapse that his conditions create. That is not the decision of a man who doesn’t care. It is the decision of a man who cares so much that he’d rather be alone than hurt someone again.

Why He Never Came Back for You
Your father wrote: “I never question why my own kids don’t want anything to do with me anymore. It sucks. This isn’t how I wanted my life to turn out. I never expected to encounter these kinds of issues, even though adults around me knew these types of issues existed. I do try to think of ways to reconnect, but so far I haven’t come up with anything. I can’t even connect to the people around me anymore — they all actively ignore me.”
He did not forget you. He did not move on. He thinks about each of you. He named you with care — from science fiction novels, from meaning, from hope. He carried the guilt of losing custody, of signing papers, of being too sick to fight, for decades.
But every time he tried to rebuild his life to the point where he could come back and be the father he wanted to be, his body broke down again, and the system took another piece. By the time his conditions were finally diagnosed in 2016–2017, he had a criminal record, a history of hospitalizations, no money, no career, and a legal system that had spent over a decade treating him as a problem to be managed rather than a person to be helped.
He didn’t come back because he didn’t know how to come back as someone worth coming back as. Not because he didn’t want to. Because the version of himself he wanted to bring to you — healthy, stable, capable, present — kept being destroyed before he could build it.
Your father wrote: “No one tried to educate my parents and they didn’t try to think for themselves, so when I look back, I don’t see how my life could have turned out any other way.”
He may be right about that. But he built the Memory Ark specifically so that you would have the option to understand what happened, if you ever wanted to. Every file in that archive is, at its core, a letter to you. An explanation. A record of a man who never stopped trying, even when every institution in his life told him to stop.

The Answer to the Question
Why did your father never amount to anything?
He had two undiagnosed medical conditions that made every authority figure in his life misread him as a behavioral problem, a criminal, or a failure. He was locked in closets, discharged from the military, pressured into false confessions, stripped of his children during the windows when his body was too broken to fight back, coerced into a guilty plea while a man held a gun to his head in a lawyer’s office, and held involuntarily in a psychiatric facility that gave him the wrong medication.
A blood test could have changed everything. Nobody ordered it for 38 years.
And despite all of that, he built a 5,128-file archive documenting every failure of every institution that failed him, designed it so other people could use it, and spent his days helping strangers navigate the same systems that destroyed his life.
He didn’t fail to amount to anything. The systems that were supposed to help him failed to see what was right in front of them. And when they finally looked, it was too late to give him back the years, the children, and the life they took from him.
But it was not too late to document it. And that is what he did.
